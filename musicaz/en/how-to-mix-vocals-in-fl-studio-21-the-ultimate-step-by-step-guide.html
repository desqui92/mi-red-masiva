How to Mix Vocals in FL Studio 21: The Ultimate Step-by-Step Guide

Mixing vocals is often considered one of the most challenging aspects of modern music production. Whether you are working on modern pop, hip-hop, reggaeton, or indie tracks, getting the lead vocal to sit perfectly in the mix requires a balance of technical precision and creative instincts. FL Studio 21 introduced powerful audio processing features, updated native plugins, and workflow enhancements that make achieving commercial-grade vocals easier than ever before.

In this comprehensive guide, we will walk you through the step-by-step process of cleaning, processing, and polishing lead vocals inside FL Studio 21 using professional mixing workflows.

Step 1: Vocal Cleaning, Gain Staging, and Pitch Correction

Before applying heavy effects, you must prepare your raw audio track. Processing poorly recorded or untimed vocals will only amplify imperfections further down your signal chain.

  • Gain Staging: Ensure your raw recording hits around -18dB to -12dB FS peak. Leaving enough headroom prevents digital distortion and allows audio plugins to operate in their optimal sweet spot.
  • Pitch Correction (NewTone): Drag your vocal clip into FL Studio’s built-in NewTone editor. Adjust pitch center and variation to correct subtle pitch inaccuracies while maintaining a natural performance.
  • Editing and De-clicking: Use FL Studio 21’s enhanced playlist fade handles and slice tools to cut out unwanted background noise, heavy breath sounds, or mouth clicks between phrases.

Step 2: Building the Essential Vocal Processing Chain

Once your vocal audio is clean and aligned, route the track to an empty channel in the FL Studio Mixer. The following sequence represents an industry-standard signal processing order for maximum clarity and control:

1. Subtractive EQ (Fruity Parametric EQ 2)

Load Fruity Parametric EQ 2 to remove unwanted resonant frequencies. Apply a high-pass filter (cutting frequencies below 80 Hz to 100 Hz) to eliminate rumble and mic stand vibrations. Perform a narrow frequency sweep to identify and attenuate harsh, boxy tones around 300 Hz - 600 Hz.

2. Taming Sibilance (De-Essing)

Harsh "s", "t", and "ch" sounds can sound painful once compressed. Use a dedicated De-esser plugin or configure Fruity Limiter in compressor mode targeting the 5 kHz - 8 kHz region to attenuate harsh sibilant spikes dynamically.

3. Dynamic Control (Compression)

Compression levels out the volume differences between quiet phrases and loud bursts. Using Fruity Limiter or Fruity Compressor, set a medium attack time (15–30 ms) and a relatively fast release (50–100 ms) with a 3:1 ratio. Aim for roughly 3dB to 6dB of gain reduction during peak sections to glue the vocal performance together.

4. Additive EQ and Saturation

With the dynamic range controlled, introduce warmth and sparkle. Use a high-shelf boost around 10 kHz for high-end "air." Follow this with subtle saturation using Fruity Fast Dist or soft-clippers to add harmonic richness, helping the vocal cut through dense instrumental arrangements.

Step 3: Creating Space with Reverb and Delay Sends

Inserting reverb and delay directly onto your primary vocal track can make the singer sound distant and wash out vocal clarity. Instead, use send buses in the FL Studio Mixer.

  • Reverb Send: Route your vocal track to an auxiliary mixer track named "Vocal Reverb." Insert Fruity Reeverb 2, set the wet parameter to 100%, and adjust the decay time (typically 1.5 to 2.5 seconds). Apply a high-pass filter on the reverb return to avoid muddying the low-mid frequencies.
  • Delay Send: Route to a second auxiliary track named "Vocal Delay." Load Fruity Delay 3, set it to a 1/4 or 1/8 note sync, and roll off both the high and low ends of the delay signal for a classic analog tape delay feel.
Pro Tip: Apply sidechain ducking to your reverb and delay tracks controlled by the clean lead vocal. This automatically lowers the volume of spatial effects while the artist is actively singing, allowing the effects to bloom seamlessly during pauses between lines.

Step 4: Parallel Compression for Density

To give your vocal track modern punch, presence, and weight without squashing its natural dynamics, employ parallel compression. Send your main vocal signal to a dedicated mixer channel, apply extreme compression (high ratio, fast attack, deep gain reduction), and blend this heavy signal beneath your clean vocal track at a lower volume.

Conclusion

Achieving professional vocal mixes in FL Studio 21 comes down to deliberate, step-by-step processing. By focusing on clean gain staging, precise subtractive EQ, controlled compression, and efficient send-routing for spatial effects, you can transform raw bedroom recordings into radio-ready vocal tracks. Apply these foundational techniques, trust your ears, and adapt each step to suit the unique characteristics of your vocalist.
